\abstract{
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards can raise in-domain scores, but its stability and transfer remain uncertain. We study Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct fine-tuning on Countdown, centered on a 22-run DAPO-style collapse screen; selected PPO- and GRPO-style checkpoints serve as observational references. Sixteen runs produced responses averaging fewer than 100 tokens by step 100. All five dynamic-sampling runs in the current environment collapsed at step 20, whereas the sole healthy dynamic-sampling run used a legacy environment, so those data confound environment with run identity. Matched no-dynamic-sampling runs varied across seeds. Selected checkpoints raised Countdown validation accuracy from 3.0\% for the base model to 64.1--75.1\%, but collapsed and healthy members of matched recipes diverged at long horizons. The largest selected-checkpoint gain in format-tolerant GSM8K accuracy was approximately three points, with no consistent improvement on MATH, BBH, or MMLU-Pro. A mandatory final-answer prompt increased the unchanged base model's strict GSM8K score by 25.2 points while reducing tolerant accuracy by 5.2 points. The evidence therefore establishes environment- and seed-dependent training instability and strong format effects, but not broad reasoning transfer or an algorithm ranking.
}

\section{Introduction}


Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a prominent approach to eliciting mathematical and symbolic reasoning from large language models (LLMs). Systems such as OpenAI o1~\cite{openai2024reasoning} and DeepSeek-R1~\cite{guo2025deepseek} demonstrate that post-training can substantially change generated reasoning behavior. However, higher task reward does not by itself establish that a model has acquired broadly transferable reasoning capability. An apparent gain can instead arise from better compliance with an answer format, a redistribution of probability toward solutions already available in the base model, or selection of an unusually successful training seed.
Community reproductions such as TinyZero made Countdown a prominent small-model testbed for R1-Zero-style training~\cite{pan2025tinyzero}; this motivates auditing whether its apparent reasoning gains are stable and transferable.

A variety of policy-optimization recipes have been proposed for LLM post-training. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) uses a learned critic and clipped policy updates~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms}. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) replaces the critic with advantages normalized over responses to the same prompt~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}. Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization (DAPO) modifies clipping and loss aggregation and uses dynamic sampling to retain prompts whose response groups contain reward variation~\cite{yu2025dapo}. These methods are usually reported with different models, datasets, reward functions, rollout budgets, and optimization schedules, so comparisons between their published headline scores do not isolate algorithmic effects.

This single-model, single-training-domain case study centers on DAPO-style stability experiments using Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Countdown; selected PPO- and GRPO-style checkpoints provide contextual, not algorithm-controlled, references. We investigate four questions: (1) how frequently early short-response collapse occurs across runs and environments; (2) whether dynamic sampling prevents collapse when training exposure is controlled; (3) how reward composition, normalization, clipping, and KL regularization relate to the survival of reasoning behavior; and (4) whether downstream gains reflect problem-solving transfer, answer-format compliance, or increased probability assigned to solutions already present in the base model's sampling distribution.

Our results reveal environment- and seed-dependent instability. A screen of 22 runs finds early short-response collapse in 16 runs, including runs with and without dynamic sampling. The dynamic-sampling subset cannot isolate a seed effect because its only healthy run is also its only legacy-environment run; within the current environment, the matched no-dynamic-sampling recipe does vary by seed. In the legacy headline comparison, disabling dynamic sampling also increased the number of trained prompts and optimizer updates by a factor of six, precluding a causal attribution to sampling alone. Under format-tolerant evaluation, the strongest individual checkpoints improve GSM8K modestly but do not consistently improve MATH, BBH, or MMLU-Pro. A prompt-only format control produces a large strict-score increase while reducing tolerant accuracy, confirming that presentation alone can move the strict benchmark substantially. These results motivate evaluating RL recipes through training stability, behavioral diagnostics, compute exposure, and held-out transfer rather than training reward alone.

Relative to the preliminary preprint~\cite{lian2025comparative}, this article adds the multi-run collapse screen, explicit environment stratification, format-tolerant rescoring, a prompt-only format control, pass@$k$ analysis, scorer-precedence corrections, and artifact manifests. It also narrows the earlier algorithm-comparison framing: PPO and GRPO are now reference recipes, and claims about dynamic sampling, training stability, and transferable reasoning are limited to what the matched evidence supports.

The remainder of this paper is structured as follows. Section~\ref{sec:related} situates the study in recent RLVR work. Section~\ref{sec:method} defines the optimization recipes, and Section~\ref{sec:methods_data} describes training and evaluation. Section~\ref{sec:results} reports training diagnostics, ablations, collapse rates, transfer, and capability coverage. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusion} discuss the scope and implications of the findings.






\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Critic-free optimization and loss normalization.}
GRPO removes the learned critic used by PPO and estimates advantages from response groups~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}. Subsequent work has shown that implementation details are consequential: REINFORCE++ uses global advantage normalization~\cite{hu2025reinforce++}, while the Dr.\ GRPO analysis identifies response-length bias induced by common GRPO normalization choices and proposes an alternative objective~\cite{liu2025understanding}. DAPO combines asymmetric clipping, dynamic sampling, token-level loss aggregation, and overlong-response handling~\cite{yu2025dapo}. These studies motivate our decision to treat named methods as complete recipes unless sampling budget, aggregation, reward, and update exposure are matched.

Reinforcement-learning reproducibility studies show that apparent improvements can depend strongly on random seeds, implementation details, and the statistical unit of analysis~\cite{henderson2018matters,engstrom2020implementation,agarwal2021precipice}. This literature motivates our separation of benchmark-item uncertainty from training-run uncertainty and our refusal to infer an algorithm ranking from single selected checkpoints.

\noindent\textbf{Sampling efficiency and collapse.}
The open-source TinyZero project used Countdown and multiplication as small-scale R1-Zero reproduction tasks~\cite{pan2025tinyzero}, helping establish the setting audited here. Dynamic sampling excludes homogeneous reward groups so that retained prompts produce a learning signal, but additional rollouts can be expensive. Selective-rollout methods instead attempt to predict and skip uninformative prompts before generation~\cite{zheng2025selectiverollouts}. A complementary line of work analyzes entropy collapse and proposes targeted clipping or KL control for high-covariance tokens~\cite{cui2025entropy}. Our study contributes a seed-level screen of a different observable failure mode---collapse to extremely short responses---and tests whether dynamic sampling, reward changes, overlong filtering, or KL regularization reliably prevent it.

\noindent\textbf{Reward validity, elicitation, and transfer.}
Recent evidence cautions against equating benchmark improvement with newly acquired reasoning. Spurious or format-based rewards can elicit large gains in some Qwen models, with weaker effects in other model families~\cite{shao2025spurious}. Large-$k$ sampling studies similarly report that RLVR often improves pass@1 while failing to expand, and sometimes narrowing, the solution coverage of the base model~\cite{yue2025reasoningcapacity}. Broader evaluations of math-tuned models find that transfer beyond the training domain is uneven and depends on the post-training method~\cite{huan2025transferability}. We connect these concerns by jointly reporting format-tolerant scores, strict-format decomposition, held-out transfer, and pass@$k$ after Countdown training.

\section{Background: Optimization Recipes}\label{sec:method}

This section reviews the formulations of PPO, GRPO, and DAPO needed to distinguish the implemented recipes; the empirical contribution begins in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Extended derivations are available in Ref.~\cite{lian2025comparative}.

PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms} is widely used for RLHF, but its learned value function adds memory and optimization overhead. GRPO~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical} and DAPO~\cite{yu2025dapo} remove the critic in favor of group-relative advantage estimation. We summarize the three objectives to identify the recipe differences examined empirically.


\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimization}


PPO replaces TRPO's constrained second-order update~\cite{schulman2015trust} with a first-order clipped surrogate objective~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms}.



\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppo_objective}
\mathcal{J}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \Big[ \min \Big( r_t \hat{A}_t,  \text{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)  \hat{A}_t \Big) \Big],  
\end{equation}


where $r_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}$ is the likelihood ratio evaluated at the sampled state--action pair $(s_t,a_t)$, $\hat{A}_t = \hat{A}^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(s_t,a_t)$ is the estimated advantage (GAE for the displayed PPO recipe~\cite{schulman2015high}), $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is the pre-update policy, and $\pi_\theta$ is the updated policy. The first argument of $\min$ is the ordinary importance-weighted policy-gradient contribution; $\operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)$ truncates the ratio to the displayed interval, where $\epsilon$ is the symmetric clipping width.


\paragraph{Clipping and regularization.}
Clipping limits extreme likelihood-ratio contributions to the surrogate objective but does not impose a hard trust region~\cite{ilyas2018deep,wang2020truly}. In our implementation, the PPO actor minimizes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total_ppo_loss}
L(\theta,\phi)=-\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)+c_1L^{\mathrm{critic}}(\phi)-c_2\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta),
\end{equation}
where $L^{\mathrm{critic}}$ trains the value model and $c_2$ controls the optional entropy bonus. The broader PPO sweep also varies a per-token KL reward penalty against the reference model; the headline PPO checkpoint in Tables~\ref{tab:headline_optimization} and~\ref{tab:headline_exposure} has that penalty disabled. Thus, the reported settings are complete recipes rather than estimates of one abstract PPO algorithm.

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimization}

For each prompt, GRPO samples $G$ responses and replaces the critic with within-group relative advantages~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grpo_advantage}
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{R_i-\overline{R}}{\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)+\epsilon_A},
\qquad \overline{R}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R_j .
\end{equation}
A conventional GRPO presentation averages the clipped token objective within each response and then across the $G$ responses. That response-normalized form is background and is not the aggregation used by the headline GRPO checkpoint. All headline group-relative runs use Verl's global \texttt{token-mean} aggregation. Defining the clipped token contribution as $\ell_{i,t}$, their implemented actor objective has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:implemented_token_mean}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{impl}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}
\left(\ell_{i,t}-\beta k_{i,t}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{i,t}=\min\!\left(r_{i,t}\widehat{A}_i,\operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat{A}_i\right)$, $m_{i,t}$ masks padding and otherwise selects generated tokens, and $k_{i,t}$ is Verl's per-token low-variance KL estimator when KL loss is enabled. For the headline DAPO recipes $\beta=0$; for the strong-KL GRPO checkpoint $\beta=0.04$. The A1 ablation additionally masks truncated responses, but A1 is not a headline checkpoint in Tables~\ref{tab:headline_optimization}--\ref{tab:headline_exposure}.

If every reward in a group is identical, Equation~\ref{eq:grpo_advantage} is zero. A small nonzero reward difference can nevertheless become an order-one standardized advantage. For example, with $G=8$, seven rewards of 0 and one format-only reward of 0.1 give $\overline{R}=0.0125$. Verl's implementation uses PyTorch's default Bessel-corrected sample standard deviation, giving $s\approx0.0354$ and a format-only advantage of $(0.1-0.0125)/(0.0354+\epsilon_A)\approx2.47$ when $\epsilon_A$ is negligible. Thus, group standardization can make a minor format credit a strong positive update signal. This calculation identifies a plausible pressure toward reward-bearing shortcuts, not a demonstrated cause of collapse. In particular, global token-mean aggregation weights generated tokens equally and does not give a short response greater weight per token; establishing the temporal mechanism requires token-level entropy and format-validity diagnostics.

\subsection{Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization}

The DAPO-style recipe uses the implemented token-mean objective in Equation~\ref{eq:implemented_token_mean} with asymmetric ratio clipping, dynamic sampling, and optional overlong-response handling~\cite{yu2025dapo}. Here the symmetric interval inside $\ell_{i,t}$ is replaced by $[1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}]$; the recorded widths are reported in Table~\ref{tab:headline_optimization}. Dynamic sampling retains prompt groups containing reward variation and resamples when too few informative groups remain. It neither balances correct and incorrect responses nor deletes correct responses from a mixed group. Global token aggregation changes length weighting; it does not create token-level rewards because all tokens share the response-level outcome advantage.

\subsection{Recipe Comparison}

PPO uses generalized advantage estimation and a learned critic; GRPO and DAPO use group-relative outcome advantages. The implementations also differ in clipping, KL placement, aggregation, group size, response cap, reward, and update exposure. We reserve causal language for explicitly matched ablations and otherwise refer to PPO-, GRPO-, and DAPO-style recipes.

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods_data}

We fine-tune Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct~\cite{qwen2.5} on Countdown and evaluate on GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}, MATH~\cite{hendrycks2021measuringmathematicalproblemsolving}, BBH~\cite{suzgun2022challenging}, and MMLU-Pro~\cite{wang2024mmlupro}. The complete test sets contain 1,319, 5,000, 6,511, and 12,032 items, respectively.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}

The \emph{standard} condition uses a benchmark-specific direct prompt and is the primary transfer measure. The \emph{think} condition requests Countdown-style reasoning and answer tags and probes whether the trained format survives out of domain. Main-table generations use vLLM greedy decoding (temperature 0), no repetition penalty, and at most 8,192 output tokens.

The strict scorer requires the answer in the last explicit final-answer marker. Format-tolerant correctness is a strict superset: a correct explicit marker is always accepted, and a marker-free fallback is applied only when generation completed without length truncation. The fallback uses the last number for GSM8K, symbolic extraction over the response for MATH, or the last choice for BBH and MMLU-Pro. Tolerant accuracy is primary; strict accuracy combines correctness and format compliance. BBH and MMLU-Pro use this custom zero-shot protocol rather than their conventional few-shot protocols.

For pass@$k$, we sample at temperature 0.6 and top-$p=0.95$ with a 4,096-token limit, drawing 64 responses per MATH-500 item and 32 per GSM8K item. We report the unbiased estimator. Paired tests compare fixed checkpoints and do not estimate training-seed uncertainty. Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation_details} gives the exact prompt strings, chat wrapping, dataset identifiers, stopping behavior, and scorer precedence. Although immutable revisions were not recorded initially, exact canonical-row comparison recovered the MATH, BBH, and MMLU-Pro revisions; their loaders are now pinned. The upstream GSM8K revision remains unknown, but the exact evaluated parquet is vendored and hashed. A machine-readable manifest and offline verifier accompany the artifact. A deterministic stratified code-and-record audit exposed two scorer-precedence errors: later incidental text could override a correct GSM8K marker, and unfinished length-truncated work could receive fallback credit. We corrected both rules, rescored the archived outputs, and added regression checks; among the five headline checkpoints, any individual benchmark accuracy changed by at most 0.91 percentage points. A blinded 40-record independent audit packet is archived but has not yet been completed, so we do not claim a human agreement or scorer-error rate. In particular, the approximately three-point tolerant GSM8K gain remains conditional on the validity of the last-number fallback.

\subsection{Training Setup and Headline Configurations}

All runs initialize from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and use Countdown as the sole training task~\cite{CountdownTasks3to4}. The DAPO-style scalar reward is the sum of a 0.1 credit for the full reasoning-and-answer tag structure and a 0.9 correctness credit. Thus a correct but format-invalid response scores 0.9, whereas a fully formatted incorrect response scores 0.1. The selected PPO and GRPO runs use an extraction-based reward: any correct extracted \texttt{<answer>} expression scores 1.0 regardless of the full reasoning-tag structure, any extracted but wrong or invalid expression scores 0.1, and a response with no extracted expression scores 0. These rewards share the numerical values 0, 0.1, and 1.0 but differ in what earns the 0.1 component and whether correct format-invalid responses score 0.9 or 1.0. Training uses Verl~\cite{sheng2024hybridflow}, one actor epoch per batch, AdamW, and rollout temperature 1.0.

\noindent\textbf{Implementation details and hardware.}
The recorded current-environment ablations used one node with four NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs (48 GB each), bfloat16 rollout weights, PyTorch 2.6.0+cu124, and vLLM 0.8.5.post1. FSDP optimization used AdamW with the framework defaults $\beta_1=0.9$ and $\beta_2=0.999$. The current-environment DAPO-style actor used weight decay 0.1, a 10-step linear warmup to the reported learning rate, and a constant schedule thereafter. The archived headline PPO and GRPO actor configurations used weight decay 0.01, zero warmup, and a constant schedule; the PPO critic likewise used weight decay 0.01 and zero warmup. The archived legacy configurations do not preserve every environment field, so current-environment specifications are not retroactively assigned to them. In the DS configuration, a group was rejected only when all eight response-level accuracy values were identical. The retry limit was non-positive (no fixed numerical cap): sampling continued until 32 informative prompt groups were collected or the finite data iterator was exhausted; the archived run required at most three generation rounds in an observed step.

A dash in Tables~\ref{tab:headline_optimization} and~\ref{tab:headline_exposure} means not applicable; NR means unavailable. Legacy headline seeds were not stored explicitly and are not inferred. The 22-run screen contains current-environment seed labels 1--6 for A1 and matched no-DS, current-environment seeds 2--6 plus one legacy run with unknown stored seeds for DS, and current-environment seeds 3--6 for A1+KL. The legacy DS run is displayed as run 1 only for table ordering; that label is not an inferred random seed. The current environment uses Verl-fork with PyTorch 2.6.0 and vLLM 0.8.5.post1. A side-by-side legacy/current record in \path{environment/DS_LEGACY_CURRENT_COMPARISON.md} distinguishes matched settings from unverified legacy fields.

\section{Results}\label{sec:results}\label{sec:parametric}

We first summarize component diagnostics and then present the collapse screen, transfer, format decomposition, and pass@$k$. Unless stated otherwise, downstream accuracies use the standard prompt and format-tolerant scoring. Most full-length configurations have one seed and do not support population-level method rankings.

\subsection{Exploratory Recipe Diagnostics}

\noindent\textbf{PPO learning rate and regularization.}
The archived PPO grid is incomplete and cannot isolate entropy or KL effects. Its directly observed downstream values are therefore reported without a component-level causal claim in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exploratory_grid}. Every evaluated PPO run at $3\times10^{-6}$ performed substantially below the base model on the four-benchmark standard-prompt mean, whereas the selected $10^{-6}$ run retained more performance. These single-seed configurations identify high-risk recipes rather than universal effects.

\noindent\textbf{GRPO group size and KL.}
The $G=2$, 4, and 8 runs generated 384, 768, and 1,536 responses per step, so group size is confounded with rollout budget and no independent group-size effect is claimed. The single-seed KL sweep is reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:exploratory_grid}; it shows a sharp prompt-conditioned contrast but does not calibrate a general threshold.

\noindent\textbf{Aggregation, DAPO group size, and dynamic sampling.}
Response-length differences cannot be attributed to aggregation because KL, reward, response cap, and rollout budget also change. DAPO group-size comparisons likewise change response budget, and $G=2$ also changes KL. The original DS/no-DS runs change both filtering and exposure. In matched-exposure runs, collapsed and non-collapsed seeds occur with and without DS; the recorded DS run also required up to three generation rounds for some steps.

\subsection{Short-Answer Shortcut under the Think Prompt}

Weak-KL GRPO runs learned a prompt-conditional shortcut: under the Countdown-style think prompt they often emitted only a final-answer tag, with median responses near 20 characters, while standard-prompt performance remained much closer to the base model. Stronger KL runs retained longer responses. This behavior is evidence that the reward can be optimized without preserving the requested reasoning trace, but the present sweep does not identify a single cause: KL, loss aggregation, reward definition, and sampling configuration differ across recipe families. The format credit also did not prevent early collapse in the multi-seed screen. We therefore report response length and prompt-conditioned behavior as diagnostics rather than treating verbosity as a proxy for reasoning quality.




\subsection{Early Short-Response Collapse across Seeds}

We use \emph{early short-response collapse} as an operational screening label, not as a direct measure of reasoning quality. The primary definition is a mean generated response length below 100 tokens over steps 90--100. Sixteen of 22 runs met this criterion. The label was often associated with answer-only behavior, but not invariably with lower Countdown validation accuracy: some endpoint-healthy no-DS seeds had validation accuracy comparable to collapsed seeds, and the longest runs also had substantial length truncation. Concise correct reasoning and long incoherent reasoning remain possible; response length alone is therefore insufficient to establish capability loss.

A threshold-sensitivity check yielded 14/22, 16/22, and 17/22 labeled runs at 50, 100, and 200 tokens, respectively. The no-DS count changed from 1/6 at 50 tokens to 3/6 at 100 and 200 tokens; DS and A1 remained 5/6 at all three thresholds, while A1+KL changed from 3/4 to 4/4 only at 200 tokens. Thus, the qualitative observation of frequent very short outputs is robust overall, but the recipe-specific no-DS rate is threshold-sensitive. The one healthy DS run was also the only DS run produced in the legacy software environment; all five DS seeds in the current environment collapsed at the same step-20 event window. Consequently, the DS data are consistent with an environment or configuration effect and do not independently establish seed sensitivity. The current-environment no-DS recipe did vary sharply by seed. The recipe-specific Wilson intervals overlap substantially, and a two-sided Fisher exact comparison of DS and matched no-DS gives $p=0.55$. With only four to six runs per arm and an environment confound, this test is severely underpowered; these data establish instability, not a statistically resolved ordering among recipes.

We also performed a descriptive post-hoc time-to-event analysis on the archived per-step histories. Five-step trailing windows were evaluated at steps 10, 15, \ldots, 100. The event time is the first window with mean response length below 100 tokens when the next two windows are also below 100; runs without an event are right-censored at step 100. As shown by the multi-panel trajectories in Figure~\ref{fig:collapse_seeds}, 17 of 22 runs had such a sustained short-response episode. The five current-environment DS events occurred by step 20; A1 events occurred at steps 25 (four seeds) and 50 (one seed); matched no-DS events occurred at steps 30, 45, and 50; and A1+KL events occurred at steps 25 or 30. One A1+KL seed subsequently recovered to a 132-token mean over steps 90--100 and was endpoint-healthy. Thus, the event denotes the first sustained episode, not an irreversible absorbing state, and it answers a different question from the primary endpoint label.

Several short-response runs retained 20--29\% Countdown validation accuracy, while the healthy A1 and legacy DS seeds reached 42\% and 51\%, respectively; the healthy no-DS seeds ranged from 19\% to 26\%. Long-response survivors could have high truncation rates (approximately 43\% for A1 seed 1 and 20\% for legacy DS seed 1), so length alone does not distinguish coherent reasoning from unfinished generation. The archived histories contain reward/score, approximate KL, and truncation, but not token entropy or an explicit format-validity metric; those missing quantities cannot be reconstructed from scalar logs.

The longer archived trajectories in Table~\ref{tab:long_horizon_countdown} show why the label matters empirically: within both current-environment matched no-DS and A1, the collapsed member remains near 29--30\% at step 520, while a healthy member reaches 63--70\%. The comparison remains observational and response length is not itself a capability measure, but the paired recipe-level divergence rules out treating every short-response endpoint as an equally capable concise style.

\subsection{Dynamic Sampling and Training Exposure}

The original DS and no-DS runs do not isolate dynamic sampling. The DS run generated approximately 200 prompts per step, retained 32, collected 256 responses, and made one optimizer update. The original no-DS run generated and trained 192 prompts, collected 1,536 responses, and made six updates per step. Thus, at step 300 the two checkpoints represent approximately 300 versus 1,800 optimizer updates. The matched no-DS ablation used 32 generated and trained prompts, 256 responses, and one update per step.

In one matched run, no-DS collapsed to a 54-token mean response and 25\% Countdown validation accuracy at step 300, whereas the legacy-environment DS run retained approximately 2,577-token responses and reached 65\%. That contrast does not support a general DS effect once the screen is considered: current-environment no-DS outcomes vary across seeds, while all five current-environment DS runs collapse. The evidence therefore does not establish that disabling DS is intrinsically superior, and it also cannot distinguish a DS-specific environment interaction from an unrecorded legacy configuration difference.

\subsection{Pass@$k$ and Solution Coverage}

Sampling-based evaluation can distinguish increased probability on sampled solutions from evidence that additional items become solvable; the empirical curves are plotted in Figure~\ref{fig:passk}. \section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The central empirical result is instability, with different evidence for environment and seed effects. Early short-response collapse occurred in 16 of 22 screened runs, often within the first 25 steps. All five current-environment DS runs failed identically at the step-20 event window, whereas the healthy DS run is from the legacy environment; this subset points to an environment or unrecorded-configuration difference rather than demonstrated DS seed variance. By contrast, the fully current-environment matched no-DS recipe shows both healthy and collapsed seeds. The selected long-reasoning checkpoints therefore come from a heterogeneous distribution containing many failures. Reporting only those checkpoints would materially overstate recipe reliability. Tables~\ref{tab:flexible_transfer} and~\ref{tab:format_decomposition} estimate performance conditional on selected checkpoints, not expected performance from starting a recipe.

Dynamic sampling cannot be assigned a simple positive or negative effect from these experiments. The original DS/no-DS comparison changes training exposure by a factor of six, while the matched and multi-seed evidence shows collapse with and without DS. Similarly, the group-size experiments increase the number of generated responses from 384 to 768 and 1,536 as $G$ increases, so group size is confounded with sampling budget. These findings are best interpreted as comparisons of complete recipes unless the varied factor and compute exposure are explicitly matched.

The transfer evidence is narrower than the preliminary preprint~\cite{lian2025comparative} implied. Under format-tolerant scoring, selected successful runs modestly improve GSM8K but do not consistently improve MATH, BBH, or MMLU-Pro. Much of the large strict-score gain is learned answer formatting. The prompt-only control strengthens that conclusion: an explicit final-line contract raised base-model strict accuracy by 25.2 points while lowering tolerant accuracy by 5.2 points. At the largest sampled $k$, successful models and the base model have similar empirical coverage, a pattern consistent with RL changing which existing behaviors are sampled first but not proving equal latent capability. This does not make the gain useless---better pass@1 can be operationally valuable---but it is different from evidence of a broad new reasoning capability.

Several limitations constrain the conclusions. All runs use one 1.5B-parameter model and one narrow arithmetic training domain. Most full-length recipes have one seed, and the healthy DS checkpoint comes from a different software environment than the later DS seeds. BBH and MMLU-Pro use a custom zero-shot protocol rather than every benchmark's conventional few-shot setting. Checkpoints are evaluated at different steps, and some configurations differ in trainer code, response cap, reward, batch construction, and update count. The format intervention is a prompt-only control and does not isolate what a format-only supervised update would do. Item-level paired tests quantify uncertainty over benchmark items for a fixed checkpoint but not over stochastic training. Full multi-seed runs in one frozen environment, a predetermined checkpoint rule, and compute-matched ablations are required before making causal or algorithm-ranking claims.

These findings suggest four practical safeguards. First, report format-tolerant correctness beside strict answer-marker scores. Second, inspect reward components and group-standardized advantages so that small format credits cannot silently dominate otherwise homogeneous groups. Third, report collapse frequency across seeds before claiming recipe superiority, using a predetermined checkpoint rule where possible. Fourth, complement pass@1 with pass@$k$ and explicit scorer audits to distinguish probability redistribution from evidence of expanded empirical solution coverage.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

Countdown RL produced large in-domain changes but did not yield uniform general-reasoning improvement in Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct. Early short-response collapse was the modal screen outcome. The DS subset is environment-confounded, while matched no-DS runs demonstrate genuine seed variation within one environment; reward changes, overlong filtering, and KL regularization also failed to prevent collapse uniformly. Among selected non-collapsed checkpoints, the clearest downstream effect was a modest GSM8K pass@1 increase and substantially better answer-format compliance; a prompt-only intervention reproduced a 25.2-point strict-score gain without a tolerant-accuracy gain. MATH, BBH, and MMLU-Pro did not improve consistently. The defensible conclusion is not that one named algorithm is best, but that environment, configuration, seed, training exposure, and evaluation protocol jointly determine the apparent outcome.

\noindent\textbf{Scorer precedence.}
For GSM8K, strict scoring selects the last occurring explicit marker among \texttt{\#\#\#\#},
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and \texttt{<answer>}. Tolerant scoring accepts a correct strict marker; otherwise it
uses the last numeric string only when the response did not end by length truncation. For MATH, strict scoring
symbolically verifies the last explicit \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} or \texttt{<answer>} content, while tolerant
scoring applies the same symbolic verifier to expressions parsed from a completed, non-length-truncated response.
For BBH and MMLU-Pro, strict scoring selects the last recognized final-answer marker and tolerant scoring
otherwise selects the last parenthesized option letter only for completed, non-length-truncated responses;
BBH additionally applies task-specific normalization for Dyck-language and word-sorting answers. The saved
per-item files contain the raw response, extracted strict and tolerant predictions, correctness flags, and
finish reason so that extraction can be re-audited without regenerating model responses.
