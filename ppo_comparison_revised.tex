\abstract{
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) can raise an in-domain score while degrading the behavior that score is intended to represent. We test this risk by fine-tuning Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on Countdown. We conduct nine 300-step runs: PPO, symmetric-clip GRPO, and asymmetric-clip DAPO-style training, with paired seeds 7--9 and matched data, reward, rollout count, actor updates, optimizer settings, and response limit. Early short-response collapse occurred in 1/3 PPO runs, 1/3 GRPO runs, and 3/3 asymmetric-clip runs. Across GSM8K, MATH, BBH, and MMLU-Pro, mean flexible accuracy under standard prompts remained near the base model (49.0\%): 49.0\% for PPO, 49.2\% for GRPO, and 48.9\% for DAPO-style training. Under the Countdown-style think prompt, the corresponding means were 43.4\%, 32.8\%, and 20.0\%, exposing prompt-conditional damage. Strict scores rose without flexible-accuracy gains; a prompt-only control likewise raised base-model strict GSM8K accuracy by 25.2 points while lowering flexible accuracy by 5.2 points. A separate 22-run configuration screen shows collapse is frequent but configuration- and run-dependent. These results do not establish a universal algorithm ranking; they show that matched multi-seed training, flexible scoring, and prompt-robust evaluation are necessary to detect reward-compatible collapse.
}

\section{Introduction}


Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a prominent approach to eliciting mathematical and symbolic reasoning from large language models (LLMs). Systems such as OpenAI o1~\cite{openai2024reasoning} and DeepSeek-R1~\cite{guo2025deepseek} demonstrate that post-training can substantially change generated reasoning behavior. However, higher task reward does not by itself establish that a model has acquired broadly transferable reasoning capability. An apparent gain can instead arise from better adherence to the required final-answer format, a redistribution of probability toward solutions already available in the base model, or selection of an unusually successful training seed.
Community reproductions such as TinyZero use Countdown as a small-model R1-Zero-style training task~\cite{pan2025tinyzero}; this motivates auditing whether its apparent reasoning gains are stable and transferable.

A variety of policy-optimization methods have been proposed for LLM post-training. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) uses a learned critic and clipped policy updates~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms}. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) replaces the critic with advantages normalized over responses to the same prompt~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}. Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization (DAPO) modifies clipping and loss aggregation and uses dynamic sampling to retain prompts whose response groups contain reward variation~\cite{yu2025dapo}. These methods are usually reported with different models, datasets, reward functions, rollout budgets, and optimization schedules, so comparisons between their published headline scores do not isolate algorithmic effects.

This single-model, single-domain study uses Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Countdown to ask four focused questions. First, when training exposure and optimization settings are matched, how often do PPO, symmetric-clip GRPO, and asymmetric-clip group-relative training meet the prespecified early-collapse criterion across paired seeds? Second, does the operational collapse label predict degraded behavior, or merely shorter answers? Third, do accuracy changes on evaluation benchmarks not used during fine-tuning persist under flexible scoring and a change of prompt format? Fourth, how do purpose-specific configuration, regularization, and evaluation controls qualify the matched comparison?

The matched experiment gives a simple central result. PPO and symmetric-clip GRPO each met the early-collapse criterion in one of three seeds, whereas asymmetric-clip DAPO-style training met it in all three. The damage was not apparent from standard-prompt flexible accuracy averaged over four evaluation benchmarks, which remained approximately 49\% for every fine-tuned model group and for the base model. It became clear under the Countdown-style think prompt: the checkpoint from early-collapse GRPO seed 9 averaged 18.2\% versus 39.8\% for the two GRPO seeds that did not meet the criterion, and the three early-collapse DAPO-style seeds averaged 20.0\%. Thus, the failure is prompt-conditional rather than a uniform loss of benchmark accuracy. A separate 22-run configuration screen (16 collapses) and a prompt-only format control show that run configuration and the required final-answer format can strongly change the apparent conclusion.

Relative to the preliminary preprint~\cite{lian2025comparative}, this article adds the matched nine-run comparison, the multi-run configuration screen, flexible rescoring, two-prompt evaluation, a prompt-only format control, pass@$k$ analysis, and reproducibility manifests. The matched runs address controlled differences among training methods; the other experiment groups address configuration stability, regularization, format sensitivity, and sampled solution coverage.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related RLVR and reproducibility work. Section~\ref{sec:method} describes the three optimization methods, Section~\ref{sec:methods_data} gives the experimental protocols, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports all experiment groups by purpose. Complete per-seed benchmark tables, exact prompts, response examples, and complete configuration-screen outcomes are provided in the appendices.






\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Critic-free optimization and loss normalization.}
GRPO removes the learned critic used by PPO and estimates advantages from response groups~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}. Subsequent work has shown that implementation details are consequential: REINFORCE++ uses global advantage normalization~\cite{hu2025reinforce++}, while the Dr.\ GRPO analysis identifies response-length bias induced by common GRPO normalization choices and proposes an alternative objective~\cite{liu2025understanding}. DAPO combines asymmetric clipping, dynamic sampling, token-level loss aggregation, and overlong-response handling~\cite{yu2025dapo}. These studies motivate our decision to treat comparisons between named methods as comparisons between complete training configurations unless sampling budget, aggregation, reward, and update exposure are matched.

Henderson et al. document seed- and environment-induced variance~\cite{henderson2018matters}; Engstrom et al. show that code-level optimizations materially change PPO and TRPO behavior~\cite{engstrom2020implementation}; and Agarwal et al. show that few-run aggregate comparisons can carry substantial statistical uncertainty~\cite{agarwal2021precipice}. This literature motivates our separation of benchmark-item uncertainty from training-run uncertainty and our refusal to infer an algorithm ranking from single selected checkpoints.

\noindent\textbf{Sampling efficiency and collapse.}
The open-source TinyZero project used Countdown and multiplication as small-scale R1-Zero reproduction tasks~\cite{pan2025tinyzero}, helping establish the setting audited here. Dynamic sampling excludes homogeneous reward groups so that retained prompts produce a learning signal, but it requires a variable number of rollouts~\cite{yu2025dapo}. Selective-rollout methods instead attempt to predict and skip uninformative prompts before generation~\cite{zheng2025selectiverollouts}. A complementary line of work analyzes entropy collapse and proposes targeted clipping or KL control for high-covariance tokens~\cite{cui2025entropy}. Our study contributes a seed-level screen of a different observable failure mode---collapse to extremely short responses---and tests whether dynamic sampling, reward changes, overlong filtering, or KL regularization reliably prevent it.

\noindent\textbf{Reward validity, elicitation, and generalization.}
Recent evidence cautions against equating benchmark improvement with newly acquired reasoning. Format, incorrect-label, and random rewards can elicit large gains in Qwen2.5-Math models, while the same signals are less reliable in other model families~\cite{shao2025spurious}. Large-$k$ sampling studies similarly report that RLVR often improves pass@1 while failing to expand, and sometimes narrowing, the solution coverage of the base model~\cite{yue2025reasoningcapacity}. Broader evaluations of math-tuned models find that generalization beyond the training domain is uneven and depends on the post-training method~\cite{huan2025transferability}. We connect these concerns by jointly reporting flexible scores, strict-format decomposition, performance on evaluation benchmarks not used during fine-tuning, and pass@$k$ after Countdown training.

\section{Background: Optimization Methods}\label{sec:method}

This section reviews the formulations of PPO, GRPO, and DAPO needed to distinguish the implemented methods; the empirical contribution begins in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Extended derivations are available in Ref.~\cite{lian2025comparative}.

PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms} is widely used for RLHF, where its learned value function adds memory and optimization overhead~\cite{hu2025reinforce++}. GRPO~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical} and DAPO~\cite{yu2025dapo} remove the critic in favor of group-relative advantage estimation. We summarize the three objectives to identify the method differences examined empirically.


\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimization}


PPO replaces TRPO's constrained second-order update~\cite{schulman2015trust} with a first-order clipped surrogate objective~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms}.



\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppo_objective}
\mathcal{J}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \Big[ \min \Big( r_t \hat{A}_t,  \text{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)  \hat{A}_t \Big) \Big],  
\end{equation}


where $r_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}$ is the likelihood ratio evaluated at the sampled state--action pair $(s_t,a_t)$, $\hat{A}_t = \hat{A}^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(s_t,a_t)$ is the estimated advantage (GAE for the displayed PPO configuration~\cite{schulman2015high}), $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is the pre-update policy, and $\pi_\theta$ is the updated policy. The first argument of $\min$ is the ordinary importance-weighted policy-gradient contribution; $\operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)$ truncates the ratio to the displayed interval, where $\epsilon$ is the symmetric clipping width.


\noindent\textbf{Clipping and regularization.}
Clipping limits extreme likelihood-ratio contributions to the surrogate objective but does not enforce a hard trust-region constraint~\cite{wang2020truly}. More broadly, empirical policy-gradient behavior can depart from the surrogate-based framework used to motivate it~\cite{ilyas2018deep}. In our implementation, the PPO actor minimizes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total_ppo_loss}
L(\theta,\phi)=-\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)+c_1L^{\mathrm{critic}}(\phi)-c_2\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta),
\end{equation}
where $L^{\mathrm{critic}}$ trains the value model and $c_2$ controls the optional entropy bonus. The matched PPO configuration sets both the entropy coefficient and the KL reward penalty to zero. Thus, the reported comparison concerns complete implemented training configurations rather than abstract algorithms.

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimization}

For each prompt, GRPO samples $G$ responses and replaces the critic with within-group relative advantages~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grpo_advantage}
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{R_i-\overline{R}}{\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)+\epsilon_A},
\qquad \overline{R}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R_j .
\end{equation}
A conventional GRPO presentation averages the clipped token objective within each response and then across the $G$ responses. That response-normalized form is background and is not the aggregation used in the matched comparison. All matched group-relative runs use Verl's global \texttt{token-mean} aggregation. Defining the clipped token contribution as $\ell_{i,t}$, their implemented actor objective has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:implemented_token_mean}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{impl}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}
\left(\ell_{i,t}-\beta k_{i,t}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{i,t}=\min\!\left(r_{i,t}\widehat{A}_i,\operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat{A}_i\right)$, $m_{i,t}$ masks padding and otherwise selects generated tokens, and $k_{i,t}$ is the Verl per-token low-variance KL estimator when KL loss is enabled. The matched GRPO and DAPO-style configurations set $\beta=0$.

If every reward in a group is identical, Equation~\ref{eq:grpo_advantage} is zero. Conversely, group standardization can amplify a small reward difference, including the 0.1 format credit used here. This supplies a plausible pressure toward reward-bearing shortcuts but is not evidence of a causal mechanism; the implementation-specific calculation is reported with the regularization diagnostics in Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}.

\subsection{Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization}

The DAPO-style method uses the implemented token-mean objective in Equation~\ref{eq:implemented_token_mean} with asymmetric ratio clipping. The symmetric GRPO interval is replaced by $[1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}]$. Published DAPO also uses dynamic sampling and overlong-response handling~\cite{yu2025dapo}; those components are disabled in the matched experiment so that the GRPO--DAPO-style contrast isolates the clipping range.

\subsection{Comparison of Training Methods}

PPO uses generalized advantage estimation and a learned critic; GRPO and DAPO use group-relative outcome advantages. Our matched comparison fixes data, reward, rollout count, actor updates, optimizer settings, aggregation, response cap, and horizon. PPO differs through its estimator and critic, while the GRPO--DAPO-style contrast isolates symmetric versus asymmetric clipping. We therefore compare the three implemented methods as a whole and do not claim that one component explains every difference.

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods_data}

We fine-tune Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct~\cite{qwen2.5} on Countdown and evaluate on four benchmarks not used during fine-tuning: GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}, MATH~\cite{hendrycks2021measuringmathematicalproblemsolving}, BBH~\cite{suzgun2022challenging}, and MMLU-Pro~\cite{wang2024mmlupro}.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:eval_protocol}

Each benchmark is evaluated with two prompt variants. The \emph{standard prompt} uses the benchmark-specific instruction; the \emph{think prompt} inserts the same item into the Countdown training wrapper and requests \texttt{<think>} and \texttt{<answer>} tags. Standard-prompt results are the primary evaluation of performance beyond Countdown. Think-prompt results test robustness to the training format. We report both prompts separately and never select the better prompt after observing the result. Exact prompt strings appear in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation_details}.

All main evaluations use vLLM greedy decoding (temperature 0), no repetition penalty, and at most 8,192 output tokens. Each model therefore produces one deterministic response per item. We score responses in two ways. The \emph{strict} scorer accepts only an answer in the required final-answer format, using the last recognized final-answer marker. The \emph{flexible} scorer accepts a correct strict-format answer and otherwise extracts an answer from completed, non-truncated text: the last number for GSM8K, symbolic matching for MATH, or the last answer choice for BBH and MMLU-Pro. Strict accuracy combines correctness with adherence to the required final-answer format; flexible accuracy is the primary approximation to problem correctness.

The complete test sets contain 1,319 GSM8K, 5,000 MATH, 6,511 BBH, and 12,032 MMLU-Pro items. BBH and MMLU-Pro use the stated custom zero-shot protocol rather than their conventional few-shot variants. Dataset revisions, chat wrapping, stopping rules, and scorer precedence are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation_details}. A deterministic code-and-record audit corrected marker precedence and removed fallback credit from length-truncated responses; the archived outputs were then rescored with regression checks. The remaining limitation of the GSM8K last-number fallback is stated in the appendix.

Pass@$k$ uses a different decoding protocol because it addresses sampled solution coverage rather than greedy pass@1 accuracy: temperature 0.6, top-$p=0.95$, a 4,096-token cap, and 64 samples per MATH-500 item or 32 per GSM8K item. Its estimator and results are reported in Section~\ref{sec:format_coverage}.

\subsection{Training Setup}

All runs initialize from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and use Countdown as the sole training task~\cite{CountdownTasks3to4}. The frozen scalar reward is 0.9 for a correct equation plus 0.1 for the complete reasoning-and-answer tag structure. A correct format-invalid response therefore scores 0.9, a fully formatted incorrect response scores 0.1, and a fully formatted correct response scores 1.0. Training uses Verl~\cite{sheng2024hybridflow}, bfloat16 weights, AdamW, one actor epoch per batch, and rollout temperature 1.0 on four NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.

\subsection{Matched Comparison of Training Methods}\label{sec:matched_design}

We froze the comparison protocol on 14 October 2026 before launching the nine runs. Each method uses the same training data, prompts, reward, software environment, paired data/rollout seeds (7, 8, and 9), and 300-step horizon. Each step samples 32 prompts with eight responses per prompt (256 trajectories) and performs eight actor updates with a response mini-batch of 32. The actor learning rate is $10^{-6}$ with a 10-step warmup and weight decay 0.1; KL loss, KL reward, and entropy regularization are disabled; loss aggregation is global token mean; and responses are capped at 4,096 tokens.

The methods differ only in their defining optimization components. PPO uses generalized advantage estimation ($\gamma=\lambda=1$), a learned critic (learning rate $10^{-5}$, mini-batch 32), and symmetric clipping 0.2/0.2. GRPO uses group-standardized advantages and symmetric clipping 0.2/0.2. The DAPO-style method uses the same group-relative estimator with asymmetric clipping 0.2/0.28. Dynamic sampling and overlong shaping are disabled, so the GRPO--DAPO-style contrast isolates clipping rather than every component of published DAPO. Actor-side exposure is matched; total compute is not identical because PPO also trains a critic.

The early-collapse criterion was fixed before training: a run was labeled as collapsed if its mean generated response length over steps 90--100 was below 100 tokens. This is an operational early-window label, not a claim that the run remains short thereafter. The cutoff was not borderline in the 22-run screen: endpoint means were 18.5--61.9 tokens for labeled collapsed runs and 132.2--2,223.7 tokens for labeled healthy runs. A later increase above the threshold is described as response-length recovery, which does not by itself imply recovery in accuracy. Every matched run continued to step 300 regardless of this label, and every seed remained in the analysis. From every run, the final checkpoint at step 300 was evaluated on the complete test sets of all four evaluation benchmarks with both prompts. With three seeds per method, means and sample standard deviations are descriptive. The single-seed KL sweeps and 22-run configuration screen use purpose-specific settings and are reported under their intended questions in Section~\ref{sec:diagnostics}; complete screen outcomes appear in Appendix~\ref{app:complete_results}.

\subsection{Prompt-Conditional Benchmark Performance}

Each method was trained in three independent runs (seeds 7--9). For the comparison, we selected the final checkpoint at step 300 from each run---one checkpoint per seed---and evaluated it on the same complete benchmark test sets. We computed accuracy separately for each checkpoint and report the mean and sample standard deviation of the three checkpoint-level accuracies in Table~\ref{tab:matched_summary}.
Under standard prompts, the fine-tuned models have nearly identical flexible accuracy to the base model: all three group means are within 0.2 percentage points of the base model's 49.0\% four-benchmark mean (Table~\ref{tab:matched_summary}). Strict standard-prompt accuracy is 4.3--6.0 points higher than the base while flexible accuracy is unchanged, indicating better adherence to the required final-answer format rather than a broad correctness gain. Standard-prompt strict-score gains were concentrated on GSM8K: mean strict accuracy was 55.6\% for PPO, 57.7\% for GRPO, and 51.9\% for DAPO-style training, compared with 33.4\% for the base model. In contrast, the corresponding flexible GSM8K accuracies were 72.2\%, 73.4\%, and 73.3\%, versus 72.3\% for the base model. MATH, BBH, and MMLU-Pro showed no consistent standard-prompt improvement. The concentration on GSM8K is consistent with partial task overlap: both Countdown and GSM8K require elementary arithmetic and a short numerical result, whereas MATH covers more varied symbolic mathematics and BBH and MMLU-Pro test broader reasoning or knowledge. Nevertheless, flexible GSM8K accuracy changed by at most 1.1 percentage points, so the large strict-score gains are better explained by more reliable final-answer formatting than by improved arithmetic reasoning. Complete per-seed benchmark values are in Appendix~\ref{app:matched_full}.

The think-prompt evaluation exposes the failure hidden under standard prompts. The checkpoint from early-collapse GRPO seed 9 averaged 18.2\%, compared with 37.8\% and 42.3\% for the two GRPO seeds that did not meet the criterion. The three early-collapse DAPO-style seeds averaged 20.0\%. PPO seed 9 was less severely affected but still fell to 36.2\%, versus 46.2--47.8\% for PPO seeds 7--8. Under conventional benchmark prompts, checkpoints from early-collapse runs retain approximately base-model accuracy. In other words, the models still answered many problems correctly under standard benchmark prompts, but the Countdown-style training prompt triggered short responses and many errors.

\subsection{Additional Training Components and Robustness Checks}\label{sec:diagnostics}

Beyond the matched comparison of PPO, GRPO, and DAPO-style training, we examined how additional training components and hyperparameters were associated with short-response collapse. These studies varied dynamic sampling, reward design, overlong-response filtering, KL regularization, and the software configuration; we also analyzed how group standardization scales the 0.1 format reward. The results below consider each training factor in turn. Because the studies use purpose-specific settings and different numbers of seeds, their runs are not pooled with the nine matched runs or interpreted as universal causal effects. The evaluation-only prompt and pass@$k$ controls are presented separately in Section~\ref{sec:format_coverage}.



\noindent\textbf{Dynamic sampling.}
In the 22-run configuration screen, 5/6 dynamic-sampling runs and 3/6 matched-exposure runs without dynamic sampling were classified as collapsed at step 100, meaning that their mean generated response length over steps 90--100 was below 100 tokens. Dynamic sampling therefore did not prevent collapse in the tested configuration; collapse was observed more often with it than without it. These small counts describe the tested runs and do not establish a general causal effect.\par

\noindent\textbf{Reward design and overlong-response filtering.}
With the correctness-only reward and overlong-response filtering (A1), 5/6 runs collapsed. Adding KL 0.01 to A1 yielded collapse in 3/4 runs. The remaining seed had a sustained short-response event at step 25 but recovered to a mean of 132.2 tokens over steps 90--100 and was labeled healthy at the endpoint. Thus, removing the 0.1 format credit and filtering overlong responses were not sufficient to prevent collapse in this screen, nor was the tested KL setting uniformly protective.\par

\noindent\textbf{Software configuration and replay.}
All five dynamic-sampling runs in the current environment collapsed near step 20. In contrast, a reconstructed legacy configuration replayed in the current environment remained healthy through step 100, averaging 1,527 tokens over steps 90--100 and reaching 47.2\% validation accuracy. This replay rules out the current software environment as a deterministic explanation, but it does not isolate the responsible random stream or residual configuration difference. The complete run-level table appears in Appendix~\ref{app:complete_results}.

\noindent\textbf{Amplification of the format reward.}
For $G=8$, seven rewards of 0 and one format-only reward of 0.1 give a mean of 0.0125. The Verl implementation uses the default Bessel-corrected sample standard deviation, $s\approx0.0354$, so the format-only response receives advantage $(0.1-0.0125)/(0.0354+\epsilon_A)\approx2.47$ when $\epsilon_A$ is negligible. This calculation shows how a small format credit can become an order-one update signal; it does not identify the cause of collapse.

\noindent\textbf{KL and response length.}
In a single-seed GRPO sweep with $G=2$, KL coefficients at or below 0.005 collapsed to approximately 20-token responses within 12 steps, whereas coefficients 0.0075 and 0.01 recovered to approximately 1,000 tokens and coefficient 0.04 remained at 270--420 tokens. A separate DAPO-style $G=2$ sweep also collapsed with KL off or $10^{-6}$ and survived with $10^{-4}$ or 0.0075. These trajectories confirm that short-response collapse can be reversible in length. Because rewards, response limits, rollout budgets, and other settings differed, these values are configuration-specific diagnostics rather than recommended thresholds, and length recovery should not be interpreted as accuracy recovery.

\subsection{Format and Coverage Controls}\label{sec:format_coverage}

The prompt-only control isolates how much strict GSM8K accuracy can move without changing model weights. We kept the base model, all 1,319 GSM8K questions, chat template, greedy decoding, 8,192-token limit, and scorer fixed. The only intervention was an instruction requiring the response to end with exactly one separate final line of the form \texttt{\#\#\#\# N}; \texttt{N} had to be replaced by only the final numeric answer, and this mandatory line had to be the last line of the response. This addition increased use of the required final-answer format from 45.6\% to 88.9\% and strict accuracy from 33.4\% to 58.5\%, while flexible accuracy decreased from 72.3\% to 67.1\% (Table~\ref{tab:format_control}). Thus, a 25.2-point strict gain can be produced by presentation alone and need not indicate improved correctness.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The matched comparison and the configuration studies answer different questions. Under the same reward, rollout count, actor-update count, optimizer settings, response cap, seeds, and 300-step horizon, early collapse occurred in 1/3 PPO runs, 1/3 symmetric-clip GRPO runs, and 3/3 asymmetric-clip DAPO-style runs. This controlled contrast is consistent with asymmetric clipping being less stable in this setting, but it does not establish a general ordering of PPO, GRPO, and DAPO: the sample contains only three seeds, PPO also changes the estimator and adds a critic, and published DAPO includes components deliberately disabled here. The configuration screen instead asks whether collapse recurs across dynamic-sampling, no-DS, reward, KL, and environment variants.

The benchmark results explain why the failure can be missed. Under standard prompts, flexible four-benchmark accuracy is essentially identical for the base model and all three fine-tuned model groups. Under the Countdown-style think prompt, checkpoints from early-collapse runs degrade sharply. The standard prompt therefore elicits residual capabilities that the think prompt suppresses, while the think prompt exposes the reward-compatible answer-only behavior learned during training. Neither prompt alone is sufficient: standard-only evaluation understates the behavioral collapse, whereas think-only evaluation confounds model quality with an unusual wrapper that already changes the base model.

Strict scores provide a second warning. Matched training raises strict standard-prompt accuracy without raising flexible accuracy, and the prompt-only intervention raises strict GSM8K accuracy by 25.2 points while lowering flexible correctness. The coverage-control pass@$k$ curves also converge at larger $k$. Together, these observations support probability and presentation changes more strongly than acquisition of broad new reasoning capability. The operational benefit of improved pass@1 or better adherence to a required output format can still be real, but it should not be described as general improvement in reasoning across tasks.

The purpose-specific experiment groups provide complementary findings rather than a single pooled comparison. The 22-run configuration screen shows that short-response collapse occurs across dynamic-sampling, no-DS, reward, and KL variants. The reconstructed legacy-configuration replay shows that the current software environment does not deterministically cause the dynamic-sampling failures. The single-seed KL sweeps show that regularization can preserve response length in some configurations but do not identify a transferable safe coefficient. Together, these results reinforce configuration and run sensitivity without resolving the stochastic mechanism.

Several limitations remain. All experiments use one 1.5B-parameter model and one arithmetic training domain. Three seeds per matched method give only descriptive estimates, and no item-level significance test can substitute for more independent training runs. The collapse definition uses response length as an operational screen rather than a direct measure of reasoning quality. The think prompt is intentionally diagnostic and nonstandard. BBH and MMLU-Pro use custom zero-shot prompts. Flexible GSM8K scoring relies partly on a last-number fallback whose independent human audit remains incomplete. Finally, actor exposure is matched, but PPO consumes additional compute for critic optimization.

Four reporting practices follow directly. First, publish every seed and use a predetermined checkpoint. Second, report flexible correctness beside strict scores. Third, evaluate both conventional prompts and the training-format prompt when a reward can create template-specific shortcuts. Fourth, treat response length, reward, and pass@1 as complementary diagnostics rather than interchangeable measures of reasoning.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In a matched nine-run Countdown experiment, PPO and symmetric-clip GRPO each met the early-collapse criterion in one of three seeds, while asymmetric-clip DAPO-style training met it in all three. Standard-prompt flexible accuracy remained at the base-model level for each fine-tuned model group, but think-prompt accuracy exposed large losses in the early-collapse group-relative runs. Strict-score gains were largely attributable to adherence to the required final-answer format, as confirmed by a prompt-only control. The configuration screen and KL diagnostics show that the phenomenon is common but configuration-dependent. The central conclusion is therefore methodological: RLVR training methods should be compared with matched multi-seed runs, explicit collapse accounting, flexible scoring, and prompt-robust evaluation. These data do not justify a universal algorithm ranking, but they do show that standard single-prompt evaluation can conceal severe reward-compatible behavioral collapse.

\noindent\textbf{Scorer precedence.}
For GSM8K, strict scoring selects the last occurring explicit marker among \texttt{\#\#\#\#},
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and \texttt{<answer>}. Flexible scoring accepts a correct strict marker; otherwise it
uses the last numeric string only when the response did not end by length truncation. For MATH, strict scoring
symbolically verifies the last explicit \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} or \texttt{<answer>} content, while flexible
scoring applies the same symbolic verifier to expressions parsed from a completed, non-length-truncated response.
For BBH and MMLU-Pro, strict scoring selects the last recognized final-answer marker and flexible scoring
otherwise selects the last parenthesized option letter only for completed, non-length-truncated responses;
BBH additionally applies task-specific normalization for Dyck-language and word-sorting answers. The saved
per-item files contain the raw response, extracted strict and flexible predictions, correctness flags, and
finish reason so that extraction can be re-audited without regenerating model responses.

A planned independent audit of 40 GSM8K records, concentrated on base-model responses credited only by the last-number fallback, was not completed. We therefore report no human agreement or fallback-error estimate; flexible GSM8K accuracy should be interpreted as conditional on this deterministic extraction rule.
