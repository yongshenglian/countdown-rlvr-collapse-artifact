\abstract{
Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) can raise an in-domain score while degrading the behavior that score is intended to represent. We test this risk by fine-tuning Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct on Countdown. We conduct nine 300-step runs: PPO, symmetric-clip GRPO, and an asymmetric-clip DAPO-style recipe, with paired seeds 7--9 and matched data, reward, rollout count, actor updates, optimizer settings, and response limit. Early short-response collapse occurred in 1/3 PPO runs, 1/3 GRPO runs, and 3/3 asymmetric-clip runs. Across GSM8K, MATH, BBH, and MMLU-Pro, mean flexible accuracy under standard prompts remained near the base model (49.0\%): 49.0\% for PPO, 49.2\% for GRPO, and 48.9\% for DAPO-style training. Under the Countdown-style think prompt, the corresponding means were 43.4\%, 32.8\%, and 20.0\%, exposing prompt-conditional damage. Strict scores rose without flexible-accuracy gains; a prompt-only control likewise raised base-model strict GSM8K accuracy by 25.2 points while lowering flexible accuracy by 5.2 points. A historical 22-run screen shows collapse is frequent but configuration- and run-dependent. These results do not establish a universal algorithm ranking; they show that matched multi-seed training, marker-independent scoring, and prompt-robust evaluation are necessary to detect reward-compatible collapse.
}

\section{Introduction}


Reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards (RLVR) has become a prominent approach to eliciting mathematical and symbolic reasoning from large language models (LLMs). Systems such as OpenAI o1~\cite{openai2024reasoning} and DeepSeek-R1~\cite{guo2025deepseek} demonstrate that post-training can substantially change generated reasoning behavior. However, higher task reward does not by itself establish that a model has acquired broadly transferable reasoning capability. An apparent gain can instead arise from better compliance with an answer format, a redistribution of probability toward solutions already available in the base model, or selection of an unusually successful training seed.
Community reproductions such as TinyZero use Countdown as a small-model R1-Zero-style training task~\cite{pan2025tinyzero}; this motivates auditing whether its apparent reasoning gains are stable and transferable.

A variety of policy-optimization recipes have been proposed for LLM post-training. Proximal Policy Optimization (PPO) uses a learned critic and clipped policy updates~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms}. Group Relative Policy Optimization (GRPO) replaces the critic with advantages normalized over responses to the same prompt~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}. Decoupled Clip and Dynamic sAmpling Policy Optimization (DAPO) modifies clipping and loss aggregation and uses dynamic sampling to retain prompts whose response groups contain reward variation~\cite{yu2025dapo}. These methods are usually reported with different models, datasets, reward functions, rollout budgets, and optimization schedules, so comparisons between their published headline scores do not isolate algorithmic effects.

This single-model, single-domain study uses Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and Countdown to ask four focused questions. First, when training exposure and optimization settings are matched, how often do PPO, symmetric-clip GRPO, and asymmetric-clip group-relative training collapse across paired seeds? Second, does the operational collapse label predict degraded behavior, or merely shorter answers? Third, do downstream changes survive both marker-independent scoring and a change of prompt format? Fourth, do broader historical ablations support the same interpretation?

The matched experiment gives a simple central result. PPO and symmetric-clip GRPO each collapsed in one of three seeds, whereas the asymmetric-clip DAPO-style recipe collapsed in all three. The damage was not apparent from standard-prompt flexible accuracy averaged over four downstream benchmarks, which remained approximately 49\% for every recipe and for the base model. It became clear under the Countdown-style think prompt: collapsed GRPO seed 9 averaged 18.2\% versus 39.8\% for the two non-collapsed GRPO seeds, and the three collapsed DAPO-style seeds averaged 20.0\%. Thus, the failure is prompt-conditional rather than a uniform loss of benchmark accuracy. A historical 22-run screen (16 collapses) and a prompt-only format control provide supporting evidence that run selection and answer format can strongly change the apparent conclusion.

Relative to the preliminary preprint~\cite{lian2025comparative}, this article adds the prospective matched nine-run comparison, the multi-run collapse screen, flexible rescoring, two-prompt evaluation, a prompt-only format control, pass@$k$ analysis, and reproducibility manifests. The matched study replaces selected historical checkpoints as the primary evidence. Historical sweeps are retained only as exploratory context.

Section~\ref{sec:related} reviews related RLVR and reproducibility work. Section~\ref{sec:method} defines the three recipes, Section~\ref{sec:methods_data} gives the matched protocol, and Section~\ref{sec:results} reports training and downstream outcomes. Secondary sweeps, complete per-seed benchmark tables, prompts, and the historical screen are provided in the appendices.






\section{Related Work}\label{sec:related}

\noindent\textbf{Critic-free optimization and loss normalization.}
GRPO removes the learned critic used by PPO and estimates advantages from response groups~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}. Subsequent work has shown that implementation details are consequential: REINFORCE++ uses global advantage normalization~\cite{hu2025reinforce++}, while the Dr.\ GRPO analysis identifies response-length bias induced by common GRPO normalization choices and proposes an alternative objective~\cite{liu2025understanding}. DAPO combines asymmetric clipping, dynamic sampling, token-level loss aggregation, and overlong-response handling~\cite{yu2025dapo}. These studies motivate our decision to treat named methods as complete recipes unless sampling budget, aggregation, reward, and update exposure are matched.

Henderson et al. document seed- and environment-induced variance~\cite{henderson2018matters}; Engstrom et al. show that code-level optimizations materially change PPO and TRPO behavior~\cite{engstrom2020implementation}; and Agarwal et al. show that few-run aggregate comparisons can carry substantial statistical uncertainty~\cite{agarwal2021precipice}. This literature motivates our separation of benchmark-item uncertainty from training-run uncertainty and our refusal to infer an algorithm ranking from single selected checkpoints.

\noindent\textbf{Sampling efficiency and collapse.}
The open-source TinyZero project used Countdown and multiplication as small-scale R1-Zero reproduction tasks~\cite{pan2025tinyzero}, helping establish the setting audited here. Dynamic sampling excludes homogeneous reward groups so that retained prompts produce a learning signal, but it requires a variable number of rollouts~\cite{yu2025dapo}. Selective-rollout methods instead attempt to predict and skip uninformative prompts before generation~\cite{zheng2025selectiverollouts}. A complementary line of work analyzes entropy collapse and proposes targeted clipping or KL control for high-covariance tokens~\cite{cui2025entropy}. Our study contributes a seed-level screen of a different observable failure mode---collapse to extremely short responses---and tests whether dynamic sampling, reward changes, overlong filtering, or KL regularization reliably prevent it.

\noindent\textbf{Reward validity, elicitation, and transfer.}
Recent evidence cautions against equating benchmark improvement with newly acquired reasoning. Format, incorrect-label, and random rewards can elicit large gains in Qwen2.5-Math models, while the same signals are less reliable in other model families~\cite{shao2025spurious}. Large-$k$ sampling studies similarly report that RLVR often improves pass@1 while failing to expand, and sometimes narrowing, the solution coverage of the base model~\cite{yue2025reasoningcapacity}. Broader evaluations of math-tuned models find that transfer beyond the training domain is uneven and depends on the post-training method~\cite{huan2025transferability}. We connect these concerns by jointly reporting flexible scores, strict-format decomposition, held-out transfer, and pass@$k$ after Countdown training.

\section{Background: Optimization Recipes}\label{sec:method}

This section reviews the formulations of PPO, GRPO, and DAPO needed to distinguish the implemented recipes; the empirical contribution begins in Section~\ref{sec:results}. Extended derivations are available in Ref.~\cite{lian2025comparative}.

PPO~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms} is widely used for RLHF, where its learned value function adds memory and optimization overhead~\cite{hu2025reinforce++}. GRPO~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical} and DAPO~\cite{yu2025dapo} remove the critic in favor of group-relative advantage estimation. We summarize the three objectives to identify the recipe differences examined empirically.


\subsection{Proximal Policy Optimization}


PPO replaces TRPO's constrained second-order update~\cite{schulman2015trust} with a first-order clipped surrogate objective~\cite{schulman2017proximalpolicyoptimizationalgorithms}.



\begin{equation}\label{eq:ppo_objective}
\mathcal{J}^{\text{CLIP}}(\theta) = \mathbb{E}_t \Big[ \min \Big( r_t \hat{A}_t,  \text{clip}(r_t, 1-\epsilon, 1+\epsilon)  \hat{A}_t \Big) \Big],  
\end{equation}


where $r_t = \frac{\pi_\theta(a_t|s_t)}{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}(a_t|s_t)}$ is the likelihood ratio evaluated at the sampled state--action pair $(s_t,a_t)$, $\hat{A}_t = \hat{A}^{\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}}(s_t,a_t)$ is the estimated advantage (GAE for the displayed PPO recipe~\cite{schulman2015high}), $\pi_{\theta_{\text{old}}}$ is the pre-update policy, and $\pi_\theta$ is the updated policy. The first argument of $\min$ is the ordinary importance-weighted policy-gradient contribution; $\operatorname{clip}(r_t,1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)$ truncates the ratio to the displayed interval, where $\epsilon$ is the symmetric clipping width.


\noindent\textbf{Clipping and regularization.}
Clipping limits extreme likelihood-ratio contributions to the surrogate objective but does not enforce a hard trust-region constraint~\cite{wang2020truly}. More broadly, empirical policy-gradient behavior can depart from the surrogate-based framework used to motivate it~\cite{ilyas2018deep}. In our implementation, the PPO actor minimizes
\begin{equation}\label{eq:total_ppo_loss}
L(\theta,\phi)=-\mathcal{J}^{\mathrm{CLIP}}(\theta)+c_1L^{\mathrm{critic}}(\phi)-c_2\mathcal{H}(\pi_\theta),
\end{equation}
where $L^{\mathrm{critic}}$ trains the value model and $c_2$ controls the optional entropy bonus. The matched PPO recipe sets both the entropy coefficient and the KL reward penalty to zero. Historical PPO sweeps vary these terms, but they are retained only as secondary diagnostics. Thus, the reported comparison concerns complete implemented recipes rather than one abstract PPO algorithm.

\subsection{Group Relative Policy Optimization}

For each prompt, GRPO samples $G$ responses and replaces the critic with within-group relative advantages~\cite{shao2024deepseekmathpushinglimitsmathematical}:
\begin{equation}\label{eq:grpo_advantage}
\widehat{A}_i=\frac{R_i-\overline{R}}{\operatorname{std}(R_1,\ldots,R_G)+\epsilon_A},
\qquad \overline{R}=\frac{1}{G}\sum_{j=1}^{G}R_j .
\end{equation}
A conventional GRPO presentation averages the clipped token objective within each response and then across the $G$ responses. That response-normalized form is background and is not the aggregation used in the matched comparison. All matched group-relative runs use Verl's global \texttt{token-mean} aggregation. Defining the clipped token contribution as $\ell_{i,t}$, their implemented actor objective has the form
\begin{equation}\label{eq:implemented_token_mean}
\mathcal{J}_{\mathrm{impl}}(\theta)=
\mathbb{E}\!\left[\frac{1}{\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}}\sum_{i,t}m_{i,t}
\left(\ell_{i,t}-\beta k_{i,t}\right)\right],
\end{equation}
where $\ell_{i,t}=\min\!\left(r_{i,t}\widehat{A}_i,\operatorname{clip}(r_{i,t},1-\epsilon,1+\epsilon)\widehat{A}_i\right)$, $m_{i,t}$ masks padding and otherwise selects generated tokens, and $k_{i,t}$ is the Verl per-token low-variance KL estimator when KL loss is enabled. The matched GRPO and DAPO-style recipes set $\beta=0$.

If every reward in a group is identical, Equation~\ref{eq:grpo_advantage} is zero. Conversely, group standardization can amplify a small reward difference, including the 0.1 format credit used here. This supplies a plausible pressure toward reward-bearing shortcuts but is not evidence of a causal mechanism; the implementation-specific calculation is given in Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}.

\subsection{Decoupled Clip and Dynamic Sampling Policy Optimization}

The DAPO-style recipe uses the implemented token-mean objective in Equation~\ref{eq:implemented_token_mean} with asymmetric ratio clipping. The symmetric GRPO interval is replaced by $[1-\epsilon_{\mathrm{low}},1+\epsilon_{\mathrm{high}}]$. Published DAPO also uses dynamic sampling and overlong-response handling~\cite{yu2025dapo}; those components are disabled in the matched experiment so that the GRPO--DAPO-style contrast isolates the clipping range.

\subsection{Recipe Comparison}

PPO uses generalized advantage estimation and a learned critic; GRPO and DAPO use group-relative outcome advantages. Our matched comparison fixes data, reward, rollout count, actor updates, optimizer settings, aggregation, response cap, and horizon. PPO differs through its estimator and critic, while the GRPO--DAPO-style contrast isolates symmetric versus asymmetric clipping. We therefore compare complete matched recipes and do not claim that one component explains every difference.

\section{Materials and Methods}\label{sec:methods_data}

We fine-tune Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct~\cite{qwen2.5} on Countdown and evaluate on GSM8K~\cite{cobbe2021gsm8k}, MATH~\cite{hendrycks2021measuringmathematicalproblemsolving}, BBH~\cite{suzgun2022challenging}, and MMLU-Pro~\cite{wang2024mmlupro}.

\subsection{Evaluation Protocol}\label{sec:eval_protocol}

Each benchmark is evaluated under two prompt conditions. The \emph{standard} condition uses the benchmark-specific instruction; the \emph{think} condition inserts the same item into the Countdown training wrapper and requests \texttt{<think>} and \texttt{<answer>} tags. Standard prompts are the primary transfer condition. Think prompts test robustness to the training format. We report both conditions separately and never select the better prompt after observing the result. Exact prompt strings appear in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation_details}.

All main evaluations use vLLM greedy decoding (temperature 0), no repetition penalty, and at most 8,192 output tokens. Each model therefore produces one deterministic response per item. We score responses in two ways. The \emph{strict} scorer accepts only the last explicit final-answer marker. The \emph{flexible} scorer accepts a correct strict marker and otherwise extracts an answer from completed, non-truncated text: the last number for GSM8K, symbolic matching for MATH, or the last answer choice for BBH and MMLU-Pro. Strict accuracy combines correctness with format compliance; flexible accuracy is the primary approximation to problem correctness.

The complete test sets contain 1,319 GSM8K, 5,000 MATH, 6,511 BBH, and 12,032 MMLU-Pro items. BBH and MMLU-Pro use the stated custom zero-shot protocol rather than their conventional few-shot variants. Dataset revisions, chat wrapping, stopping rules, and scorer precedence are documented in Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation_details}. A deterministic code-and-record audit corrected marker precedence and removed fallback credit from length-truncated responses; the archived outputs were then rescored with regression checks. The remaining limitation of the GSM8K last-number fallback is stated in the appendix.

Pass@$k$ is secondary because it uses a different decoding protocol: temperature 0.6, top-$p=0.95$, a 4,096-token cap, and 64 samples per MATH-500 item or 32 per GSM8K item. Its estimator and results are given in Appendix~\ref{app:secondary}.

\subsection{Training Setup}

All runs initialize from Qwen2.5-1.5B-Instruct and use Countdown as the sole training task~\cite{CountdownTasks3to4}. The frozen scalar reward is 0.9 for a correct equation plus 0.1 for the complete reasoning-and-answer tag structure. A correct format-invalid response therefore scores 0.9, a fully formatted incorrect response scores 0.1, and a fully formatted correct response scores 1.0. Training uses Verl~\cite{sheng2024hybridflow}, bfloat16 weights, AdamW, one actor epoch per batch, and rollout temperature 1.0 on four NVIDIA RTX A6000 GPUs.

\subsection{Matched Recipe Comparison}\label{sec:matched_design}

We froze the comparison protocol on 14 October 2026 before launching the nine runs. Each recipe uses the same training data, prompts, reward, software environment, paired data/rollout seeds (7, 8, and 9), and 300-step horizon. Each step samples 32 prompts with eight responses per prompt (256 trajectories) and performs eight actor updates with a response mini-batch of 32. The actor learning rate is $10^{-6}$ with a 10-step warmup and weight decay 0.1; KL loss, KL reward, and entropy regularization are disabled; loss aggregation is global token mean; and responses are capped at 4,096 tokens.

The recipes differ only in their defining optimization components. PPO uses generalized advantage estimation ($\gamma=\lambda=1$), a learned critic (learning rate $10^{-5}$, mini-batch 32), and symmetric clipping 0.2/0.2. GRPO uses group-standardized advantages and symmetric clipping 0.2/0.2. The DAPO-style recipe uses the same group-relative estimator with asymmetric clipping 0.2/0.28. Dynamic sampling and overlong shaping are disabled, so the GRPO--DAPO-style contrast isolates clipping rather than every component of published DAPO. Actor-side exposure is matched; total compute is not identical because PPO also trains a critic.

Thus, the training did not uniformly erase the model's underlying capabilities; it created a prompt-triggered behavioral failure.

\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

The matched experiment changes the interpretation of the earlier evidence. Under the same reward, rollout count, actor-update count, optimizer settings, response cap, seeds, and 300-step horizon, early collapse occurred in 1/3 PPO runs, 1/3 symmetric-clip GRPO runs, and 3/3 asymmetric-clip DAPO-style runs. This result is more informative than comparing selected historical checkpoints trained with different budgets. It is consistent with asymmetric clipping being less stable in this setting, but it does not establish a general ordering of PPO, GRPO, and DAPO: the sample contains only three seeds, PPO also changes the estimator and adds a critic, and published DAPO includes components deliberately disabled here.

The downstream results explain why the failure can be missed. Under standard prompts, flexible four-benchmark accuracy is essentially identical for the base model and all three recipes. Under the training-format think prompt, collapsed checkpoints degrade sharply. The standard prompt therefore elicits residual capabilities that the think prompt suppresses, while the think prompt exposes the reward-compatible answer-only behavior learned during training. Neither condition alone is sufficient: standard-only evaluation understates the behavioral collapse, whereas think-only evaluation confounds model quality with an unusual wrapper that already changes the base model.

Strict scores provide a second warning. Matched training raises strict standard-prompt accuracy without raising flexible accuracy, and the prompt-only intervention raises strict GSM8K accuracy by 25.2 points while lowering flexible correctness. The historical pass@$k$ curves also converge at larger $k$. Together, these observations support probability and presentation changes more strongly than acquisition of broad new reasoning capability. The operational benefit of improved pass@1 or format compliance can still be real, but it should not be described as general reasoning transfer.

The historical evidence is supportive rather than primary. The 22-run screen shows that short-response collapse occurs across dynamic-sampling, no-DS, reward, and KL variants. The reconstructed legacy replay shows that the current software environment does not deterministically cause the dynamic-sampling failures. The single-seed KL sweeps show that regularization can preserve response length in some configurations but do not identify a transferable safe coefficient. These results reinforce configuration and run sensitivity without resolving the stochastic mechanism.

Several limitations remain. All experiments use one 1.5B-parameter model and one arithmetic training domain. Three seeds per matched recipe give only descriptive estimates, and no item-level significance test can substitute for more independent training runs. The collapse definition uses response length as an operational screen rather than a direct measure of reasoning quality. The think prompt is intentionally diagnostic and nonstandard. BBH and MMLU-Pro use custom zero-shot prompts. Flexible GSM8K scoring relies partly on a last-number fallback whose independent human audit remains incomplete. Finally, actor exposure is matched, but PPO consumes additional compute for critic optimization.

Four reporting practices follow directly. First, publish every seed and use a predetermined checkpoint. Second, report marker-independent correctness beside strict scores. Third, evaluate both conventional prompts and the training-format prompt when a reward can create template-specific shortcuts. Fourth, treat response length, reward, and pass@1 as complementary diagnostics rather than interchangeable measures of reasoning.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}

In a matched nine-run Countdown experiment, PPO and symmetric-clip GRPO each collapsed in one of three seeds, while the asymmetric-clip DAPO-style recipe collapsed in all three. Standard-prompt flexible accuracy remained at the base-model level for every recipe, but think-prompt accuracy exposed large losses in the collapsed group-relative runs. Strict-score gains were largely attributable to answer-format compliance, as confirmed by a prompt-only control. Historical screens and KL sweeps show that the phenomenon is common but configuration-dependent. The central conclusion is therefore methodological: RLVR recipes should be compared with matched multi-seed runs, explicit collapse accounting, marker-independent scoring, and prompt-robust evaluation. These data do not justify a universal algorithm ranking, but they do show that standard single-prompt evaluation can conceal severe reward-compatible behavioral collapse.

\noindent\textbf{Scorer precedence.}
For GSM8K, strict scoring selects the last occurring explicit marker among \texttt{\#\#\#\#},
\texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}}, and \texttt{<answer>}. Flexible scoring accepts a correct strict marker; otherwise it
uses the last numeric string only when the response did not end by length truncation. For MATH, strict scoring
symbolically verifies the last explicit \texttt{\textbackslash boxed\{\}} or \texttt{<answer>} content, while flexible
scoring applies the same symbolic verifier to expressions parsed from a completed, non-length-truncated response.
For BBH and MMLU-Pro, strict scoring selects the last recognized final-answer marker and flexible scoring
otherwise selects the last parenthesized option letter only for completed, non-length-truncated responses;
BBH additionally applies task-specific normalization for Dyck-language and word-sorting answers. The saved
per-item files contain the raw response, extracted strict and flexible predictions, correctness flags, and
finish reason so that extraction can be re-audited without regenerating model responses.

A planned independent audit of 40 GSM8K records, concentrated on base-model responses credited only by the last-number fallback, was not completed. We therefore report no human agreement or fallback-error estimate; flexible GSM8K accuracy should be interpreted as conditional on this deterministic extraction rule.

\section{Secondary Historical Analyses}\label{app:secondary}

\noindent\textbf{Standardized format reward.}
For $G=8$, seven rewards of 0 and one format-only reward of 0.1 give a mean of 0.0125. The Verl implementation uses the default Bessel-corrected sample standard deviation, $s\approx0.0354$, so the format-only response receives advantage $(0.1-0.0125)/(0.0354+\epsilon_A)\approx2.47$ when $\epsilon_A$ is negligible. This calculation shows how a small format credit can become an order-one update signal; it does not identify the cause of collapse.

\noindent\textbf{KL and response length.}
In a single-seed GRPO sweep with $G=2$, KL coefficients at or below 0.005 collapsed to approximately 20-token responses within 12 steps, whereas coefficients 0.0075 and 0.01 recovered to approximately 1,000 tokens and coefficient 0.04 remained at 270--420 tokens. A separate DAPO-style $G=2$ sweep also collapsed with KL off or $10^{-6}$ and survived with $10^{-4}$ or 0.0075. Because rewards, response limits, rollout budgets, and other settings differed, these values are recipe-specific diagnostics rather than recommended thresholds.

\noindent\textbf{Historical collapse screen.}
The complete 22-run table in Appendix~\ref{app:complete_results} includes dynamic sampling, matched-exposure no-DS, correctness-only reward with overlong filtering (A1), and A1 with KL 0.01. Sixteen runs met the primary endpoint. The five current-environment dynamic-sampling runs collapsed near step 20, while a reconstructed legacy-configuration replay in the same environment remained healthy through step 100 (mean 1,527 tokens; 47.2\% validation accuracy). The replay rules out deterministic environment failure but does not isolate the responsible random stream or residual configuration difference.

\noindent\textbf{Pass@$k$.}
For MATH-500, flexible pass@1 ranged from 54.1\% to 55.2\% and pass@64 from 90.8\% to 92.2\% across the base, healthy DS, original no-DS, and strong-KL GRPO checkpoints. For GSM8K, pass@1 ranged from 70.4\% to 73.9\% and pass@32 from 96.3\% to 97.1\%. These selected historical checkpoints use a different sampling protocol and are not part of the matched method comparison.
